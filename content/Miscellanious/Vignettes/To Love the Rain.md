Once, somewhere in the hills of [[Strioc an Athraithe|Athraithe]] (before it was called Athraithe) there lived a farmer. He lived a simple life, but a happy one - he was kind to the land, and it was kind to him. He was kind to his animals and to his neighbors, he took only what he needed and he gave what he did not need to those who did. He was content, but he was lonely. He had worked hard to build his farm and his life, but found that once he'd done so, he had no one to share it with. 

That is, until one day, when his farmstead was in the midst of a summer shower. He was out in his field, guiding his cows to shelter, when he saw a figure moving amongst the raindrops. She was the most beautiful woman he had ever seen - she had deep azure hair that glimmered like a gemstone, and fair skin that seemed to glow as the water ran down it. She danced through the rain without a care in the world, with no regard for the cold or the wet, and the farmer was instantly entranced.

He approached her where she danced near the edge of his fields, calling out when he got near. "Hail, miss! Are you lost? Do you need someplace to stay?"

The woman stopped her dance and looked to him with blue eyes as deep as the sea. She looked curiously at him, and stepped forward. She shook her head, though which question she was answering the farmer did not know.

Though he was stunned by the beauty of her eyes, he eventually managed to speak once more. "I'd hate to see you catch a cold," he said to her. "Please, take this," he said as he held out his wide straw hat. The raindrops struck his uncovered head, but he found as he held out the hat that the water felt soothing and comfortable.

The woman looked curiously at the offered hat for a moment, then smiled and whisked it from the farmer's hand. Without a word, she placed it on her head and danced away, through the rain and off into the hills. The farmer watched her go, equally perplexed and charmed.

Some time passed, but the farmer could not forget the strange woman in the rain. Perhaps that is why, when the next shower visited his farm, he was sitting on his porch, looking out into the sheets of rain. And lo, soon before him he saw a graceful figure dancing down the path to his door. It was the woman again, and held in her hands was the very hat he had given her when last they met.

She made her way to where the farmer stood and placed the hat in his hands, then placed a kiss on his cheek. "A gift returned in the spirit it was offered," she said in a voice like a babbling brook. The farmer glanced down at it, and saw that it was mended from the ragged state it had been in before. Not only this, but he would later find that it perfectly protected his face against any rain, no matter how strong.

But he did not pay any mind to the hat at that moment, for he was entranced by the mirth in her voice, the softness of her lips against his cheek, and as she stepped under the porch roof, he saw for the first time her beauty un-obscured by the rain.

"O-Once again miss," he stammered, "I must offer you shelter if you need it. But if not... would you like to share some tea, perhaps?"

The woman looked at him with an expression of pleasant bewilderment, then shrugged and gracefully sank into a chair on the porch. "Perhaps I would," she said to the farmer with a smile.

The two shared many cups of tea and talked at length about any number of topics. Though she spoke not of her past, the farmer found that this woman had traveled all across Antir, and seen wonders he could barely imagine. Despite that, she seemed astounded by the parts of his life that he thought most mundane. And though she had spoken little when they first met, he found that she was the best company he had ever met. By the time the rain began to lessen, and the woman stood to leave, the farmer found that he was undeniably and completely in love.

"Might I see you again?" he asked her.

"You might," she replied with a smile, and gave him another kiss on the cheek before departing.

He did, in fact, see her again. Every time it rained on his little home, he would sit on the porch, and she would visit. The pair would share stories, and games, and eventually the farmer began to hope that she shared the feelings that had now grown so strong in him - though he never dared voice those feelings, or ask if they were returned.

This continued for many months. Then, the next summer, a drought struck the land in which he lived. Weeks passed without a single drop of rain, and the farmer began to fear that he would never see the woman he loved again.

So he went down to the village near which he lived, and found the village priest. He pleaded with them, asking how he might entreat the gods to bring rain once more. The priest told him that Tarbh, god of nature's bounty, may answer his prayers if he proved himself worthy through service. 

So the farmer found a field of wheat, belonging to his neighbor, which was suffering greatly from the drought. He fetched a bucket and hauled water from the nearby river up to the field, watering each stalk of grain. He did this across the entire field, until one thousand times he had paid his respects to Tarbh through his labor.

As he knelt down beside the newly watered field, he heard a whisper in the wind through the grain. "My child," the voice said, for it was Tarbh himself. "I have heard your prayers. Your need is fair, but I cannot grant your wish. The rains are needed elsewhere, and were I to move them many may perish. Go with my blessing, and know that all will be right in time."

The farmer returned to his stead, but after another week without rain he found he could not wait for the god's words to come true. So he found his way to the darkened woods, where a witch was known to live. He walked for a great while, across many roads and trails, until he had traveled one thousand twisting paths searching for the witch's abode. Finally, he found a humble hut nestled within a grove of thorn. 

The witch emerged from within, an ancient elven man with leaves in his hair and bones around his neck. "What do you seek, traveler, that you would walk so many paths to reach me?"

The farmer explained his plight, and the witch regarded him mournfully. "Your need sounds dire, sir, but I am afraid I may not be of assistance. I know all that a mortal can know in the ways of the darkness and the passing of souls, but the moving of the natural world is beyond my grasp."

The farmer thanked him for his time and departed, returning once more to his home. But he had waited so long that he could not stand even another day, so that very evening he set out towards the eastern mountains. There he knew he could find a powerful wizard, scrying the heavens atop her mountain tower. He reached the base of the mountain, and climbed one thousand flights of steps to reach the very top, where the wizard had built her tower.

The wizard emerged, shocked that a mere farmer such as he had traveled such lengths. "You have earned favor by will alone, sir, what might I do for you?"

He once more explained his plight, and the wizard sighed. "I am sorry, my good man, but the natural world is barred from my magics. Were I to try I might lose my magic, or my very life. I can move the earth and rain fire from the sky, but the weather, as all natural things, is beyond my domain."

Mournfully, the farmer returned home. He knelt in his field and, left with no recourse, he cried out one thousand times to the sky, begging the rains to return so he might see his love once more.

Finally, a voice returned his call. "Please, friend, stop!" it cried. "You're scaring away the birds!" But the voice was not from the sky - instead, it was from a traveler walking down the road. "Hark, mournful stranger," the man continued, "are you the darling of a lovely woman with azure hair?"

The farmer looked at the stranger with bewilderment. The man was dressed in little more than rags, wearing a cloak of fur and holding a gnarled staff draped with vines.

"Why yes, I am," replied the farmer, still baffled. "I have been long searching for her, but we are in a drought, and-"

The farmer was cut off as the stranger exclaimed, "Thank the stars! I've found you at last!" With this, he raised his staff to the sky and chanted strange words, and the farmer rejoiced as a few small clouds began to appear in the sky above.

Heavy drops began to fall, and as the farmer watched, his beloved appeared from thin air, as if stepping out from behind the curtain of rain. She let out a cry of joy and leapt to his arms, and the pair held a long embrace. She kissed him, and he knew in that moment that all his feelings towards her were returned.

As he held her, being soaked by the rain and completely uncaring, he turned to the ragged stranger, who stood dry just beyond the tiny raincloud. "Who might you be, my good sir? How might I repay you for this invaluable service?"

The man shook his head with a weary smile. "You needn't pay me at all, my friend, for I am a druid. It is my duty to tend to the spirits and their needs, and your beloved has been begging me to reunite the two of you for weeks now."

The farmer looked at him, not understanding the man's words, and the druid continued. "My good man, your dearest is a Nephele, a spirit of the rain. She is of the fae. Surely you knew this?"

The farmer grew embarrassed, for he had not realized his love was anything but a mortal woman with a fondness for rain. The woman, still held in his arms, laughed mirthfully and kissed his cheek. "My love," she said, "If I were not bound to the rains I would never have left you."

This heartened the farmer, but saddened him as well - for he knew this temporary rain would not last. As if reading his thoughts, the druid produced a small stone from his pocket and handed it to the farmer. Within the center of a stone was a crystal, where small lights moved up and down like an eternal shower of glowing rain.

"This will be a bridge between our world and hers," the druid explained. "Keep it with you and she will never be forced from your side again."

The farmer rejoiced, and as the raincloud faded, his love remained with him, beaming at his side. He thanked the druid profusely, and offered him a place at his hearth, but the ragged man departed to continue his service to the spirits. The farmer and his love returned back to his stead, where they lived happily for the rest of the farmer's days.

Some say that they day he died, a new cloud formed in the sky over his farmstead. If ever you see a pair of rainclouds chasing each other through the sky, perhaps it is the farmer and his love, dancing forever in the otherworld.
